# Hedging and claim strength

**Inputs:** the design, sample and uncertainty, replication, alternative explanations, and the intended claim.

## Procedure

1. Separate three levels: the observed result, the interpretation within the studied system, and the generalisation beyond it.
2. State the observation directly, with numbers.
3. For interpretation, pick a verb or modal that matches the support: "is consistent with", "suggests", "may indicate", "indicates", "shows", "demonstrates", from weaker to stronger.
4. Use tense to calibrate. Present simple offers a finding as a general fact; past simple ties it to this study. See [tense](../tense-selection/SKILL.md).
5. Limit scope with the population, setting, duration, frequency, or quantity ("in 8 of 10 cases", "at the tested sites").
6. Fit the strength to the section: Results report observations; Discussion carries interpretation and hedges; Abstract and Conclusion state supported findings plainly; Title stays neutral; future work uses modals. Keep the strength consistent across sections, using "may" in the Abstract only if the paper also qualifies it.
7. If a result is strong, do not bury it under several vague hedges.

## Guardrails

Hedging is calibration, not evasion. Do not weaken a numerical finding to hide it or strengthen an association into causation. Avoid "proves" for ordinary empirical findings. Do not describe a result as "clear" or "obvious" if it is not.

## Check

Would the sentence hold if the most plausible alternative interpretation were true? Does the sentence tell the reader how far the inference reaches?

**Illustrative example:** "The measured error declined at two sites" is an observation. "These data suggest that recalibration could improve short-term accuracy at similar sites" is a bounded interpretation.
