# Evaluative language

Results do not speak for themselves. A reader who sees only a number forms an opinion of it, and may disagree with the author's. Telling readers what you think the numbers mean, and why, keeps the conclusions from feeling surprising.

## Three levels

| Level | Example | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Objective | "Error fell from 12% to 7%" | Report the value |
| Evaluative | "Error fell considerably, from 12% to 7%" | Say how big, for whom |
| Interpretive | "...in line with the laboratory prediction" | Say what it means against a benchmark |

Objective wording alone often adds nothing the reader cannot already see in the figure, so a pure restatement is rarely the best use of a sentence.

## Procedure

1. For each key number, choose a benchmark: a prediction, a prior study, a threshold, a control, or practical relevance.
2. Frame the number against it. "As many as 23% of cases" and "only 23% of cases" describe the same data differently, so choose the frame the benchmark supports.
3. Match intensity to the size of the difference: slightly, noticeably, markedly. Use one criterion across the paper so the same gap always gets the same wording.
4. Use frequency and quantity scales for precision. See [frequency](../frequency-language/SKILL.md) and [quantity](../quantity-comparisons/SKILL.md).
5. Highlight what matters by selecting. Describe a few results in detail, mark the important or unexpected one with a new paragraph and an early contrast signal.
6. Express strong results with precise words (striking, marked, consistent) rather than exclamation marks. Keep strong adjectives for strong evidence.
7. Keep explanation, evaluation, and implication separate: why it happened, what the numbers mean, what they suggest.

## Guardrails

Framing must be defensible. Do not pick an adjective to persuade against the data. Do not call a result "typical" unless it is. Do not use "significant" except in its statistical sense. If a journal requires purely descriptive Results, report objectively and move evaluation to the Discussion.

## Check

Would a reader with only the number reach the same view as the author? Does the same size of effect receive the same wording everywhere? Is every evaluation traceable to a stated benchmark?

**Illustrative example:** Flat: "The effect occurred in 23% of cases." Framed: "The effect occurred in as many as 23% of cases, well above the 5% reported for the earlier method." (Use only if the benchmark is supplied.)
