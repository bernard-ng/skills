# Title

**Inputs:** the research question, design and scope, key variables or systems, the strongest result the paper supports, journal style, and likely search terms.

More people read the title than the abstract, and many more read the abstract than the paper. A title should attract the right readers and predict the paper accurately.

## Procedure

1. Turn the research question into a noun phrase: "What differs between X and Y?" becomes "A comparison of X and Y"; "How does X affect Y?" becomes "The effect of X on Y".
2. Write three candidates emphasising the question, the method or setting, and the key supported result. Choose the one that best matches the paper and journal.
3. Reflect scope: a case study names the case; a survey names the class. "Crack propagation in a pressurised pipe" is narrower than "Crack initiation in pressurised pipes".
4. Include the key words readers would search for. Delete generic openers such as "A study of" or "An investigation into".
5. Keep it concise, formal, and usually in sentence case. Questions and exclamation marks are uncommon.
6. Check every noun stack and preposition by paraphrasing the relationship each one expresses. See [noun phrases and prepositions](../noun-phrases-prepositions/SKILL.md).
7. Name the specific systems, substrates, species, or conditions actually studied, not the wider class.
8. Check the journal's length, capitalisation, abbreviation, and punctuation rules. Avoid abbreviations unless universally understood.

## What a title should and should not carry

State content neutrally. A result-led title is acceptable when the result is a real, directly supported achievement. Titles rarely carry value words ("reliable", "improved") or modals ("may", "could"); caveats belong in the abstract and paper. Do not set expectations the paper does not meet.

## Guardrails

Do not promise a broader population, mechanism, or causal claim than the article supports. Do not use unexplained abbreviations. Do not let the title carry caveats that belong in the abstract.

## Check

Does the title overstate generality, causation, or novelty? Does it specify the actual population or system? Could a noun stack or preposition suggest another reading? Does each key term appear in the paper? Would a reader searching for this topic find it?

**Illustrative options:** "Seasonal drift of low-cost air monitors at roadside sites" or, if directly demonstrated, "Calibration reduces drift in low-cost air monitors during short deployments."
