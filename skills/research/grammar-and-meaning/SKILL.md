# Grammar and meaning

**Inputs:** the draft, the intended scientific meaning, and the journal style.

The most damaging errors are invisible: the sentence is grammatical, so no editor flags it, but it does not say what the author meant. Check meaning as well as form. A proofreader who does not know the science cannot find these.

## Procedure

1. State each sentence's proposition and its job (background, procedure, result, interpretation).
2. Check the forms that change meaning without looking wrong: tense, articles, modifier and adverb placement, prepositions, relative clauses, compound nouns, comparisons, and citation placement.
3. Check agreement, countability, pronoun reference, parallel structure, negation, and units.
4. Check relative clauses: a clause that restricts the noun ("the samples that were heated") differs from one that adds information ("the samples, which were heated"). Punctuation changes the claim.
5. Prefer common sentence patterns used in the field over literal translations from another language. Conventional wording is safer and easier to read than inventive wording.
6. Revise minimally, then explain any edit that changes meaning and ask the author to confirm.

## Guardrails

Do not silently strengthen findings or replace a technical term without checking field meaning. Preserve symbols and values exactly.

## Check

Can a reader identify what acted on what, under which conditions, with which certainty? Is each comparison grammatically and scientifically parallel?

**Illustrative example:** "The treatment reduced error more than the control" may compare the treatment with a group. If the comparison is the size of the reduction, write "The reduction in error was greater in the treatment group than in the control group."
