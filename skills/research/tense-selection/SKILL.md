# Tense choice

Tense is part of the claim. A grammatical tense can still say the wrong thing, and editors rarely notice, so check it.

## Core contrasts

- **Present simple** states what the writer treats as established: accepted background, general truths, what a figure or paper currently shows. Which findings count as established is the author's judgement.
- **Past simple** ties a statement to a completed event: a cited study, a procedure performed, a result observed.
- **Present perfect** links earlier events to the present: accumulated research, a current state, a gap that still exists. What matters is relevance now, not how long ago.

## By section

| Section and job | Default |
| --- | --- |
| Introduction: accepted background, importance | Present simple |
| Introduction: amount of recent work, gap still open | Present perfect |
| Introduction: a named study | Past simple (or present perfect for a line of work) |
| Introduction: the paper itself | Present simple ("This paper tests...") |
| Introduction: the aim | Past simple, or present simple if only partly achieved here |
| Methods: standard procedure or equipment | Present simple (often passive) |
| Methods: what was done in this study | Past simple (often passive) |
| Results: observations | Past simple |
| Results: interpretation | Past simple (local) or present simple (treated as established) |
| Discussion: recalled method or results | Past, present perfect, or present simple |
| Conclusion and Abstract: achievement | Present perfect or present simple |
| Abstract: gap and what the paper does | Present simple |
| Applications | Present simple with can or may |

## Procedure

1. Label each main verb by its job (background, prior study, gap, procedure, observation, interpretation, achievement, application).
2. Choose the tense that fits the job, then check whether it implies the right time, actor, and scope.
3. Treat each shift as meaningful and verify it matches a change of function: cited past work to a current gap, standard method to own procedure, observation to interpretation.
4. Decide interpretation strength deliberately. "We found that X increased, which suggested Y" keeps the claim local to this study. "We found that X increases, which suggests Y" offers it as a general truth, so do it only when the evidence supports that.
5. Do not mix tenses randomly in one paragraph.

## Guardrails

These are defaults; field and journal conventions vary. Do not switch tense mechanically, and do not use present simple where it would make one study's result sound universal.

## Check

If the tense changed, would the sentence mean something else? Does the verb imply the right time, actor, and claim scope?

**Illustrative example:** "Lee measured drift in 2022 (past, one study). Later work has tested the same device indoors (present perfect, relevant now), but its outdoor stability remains uncertain (present, the gap)."
