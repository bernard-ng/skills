# Discussion and Conclusion

**Inputs:** the aims, the main results, the key studies already cited, plausible mechanisms and alternatives, real limitations, genuine applications, and the next study the limits suggest. Journals vary on combined or separate Discussion and Conclusion; a Conclusion is often one or two short paragraphs.

## Four-move model

This section widens from the study to the field, mirroring the Introduction's narrowing.

1. **Revisit a previous section.** Open with the most significant element, not a random one: the gap or aim if answering it is the main contribution; the method if the method is the contribution; the results if they confirm a theory or reveal something new. Reuse the earlier wording as an echo so readers recall it. Summarise the key results as a bridge. Do not add new method details.
2. **Map the study onto existing research.** Show whether it confirms, extends, refines, contradicts, or takes a different route from earlier work. Use studies already introduced. See [contribution mapping](../contribution-mapping/SKILL.md).
3. **State the contribution and refine its implications.** Say what the results achieve, explain why or how (a mechanism or reason), consider other interpretations, address unexpected results, and note applications where they exist. Do not restate results in other words.
4. **Limitations, future work, applications.** Pick limitations that point to concrete next steps. End with the supported message, an application, or an invitation to future work. See [limitations and future work](../limitations-future-work/SKILL.md).

## Procedure

- For each key result, record the claim it supports, competing interpretations, and the comparison study.
- Recall your own method or results in past, present perfect, or present simple, depending on whether you are describing what was done or what the paper does. Present perfect fits a short Conclusion ("We have shown").
- Separate the finding, the interpretation, and the speculation, and choose modals and verbs accordingly. See [modal verbs](../modality/SKILL.md), [claim strength](../claim-strength/SKILL.md), and [causality](../causal-language/SKILL.md).
- State achievements plainly and positively, using the strength the evidence earns. See [evaluative language](../evaluative-language/SKILL.md).
- Check for applications at the start of the Introduction and in similar papers. Some work has none, and none should be invented.
- Make future work specific. "Should" invites others; "will" announces your plan.
- More wording in the [phrase bank](references/discussion-language.md).

## Guardrails

Do not turn association into causation, novelty into priority, or a within-study observation into a universal claim. Do not claim "first" without a thorough search, and keep "to our knowledge" for the residual risk. Do not introduce a limitation here that Methods and Results ignored.

## Check

Does the section answer the stated aim? Does it separate finding, interpretation, and speculation? Are novelty and application claims bounded? Do the future-work suggestions follow from real limitations? Does the ending give the reader the supported take-away?

**Illustrative example:** "Calibration reduced median error at two of three sites, as the earlier laboratory studies predicted. Because only one season was sampled, the benefit in other climates is uncertain. A multi-season comparison should test that boundary."
