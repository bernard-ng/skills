# Results

**Inputs:** verified numbers, units, sample sizes, statistical output, figure and table labels, the aims and predictions they answer, prior findings to compare against, and unexpected or problematic results.

Results do not speak for themselves. A figure already shows the numbers; the prose must say which findings matter, what they mean against the aim, and how they relate to earlier work. Report observations separately from explanations and implications. See [evaluative language](../evaluative-language/SKILL.md).

## Four-part menu

Select what suits the study and journal. Combined "Results and Discussion" sections take more of the interpretation here.

1. **Orient the reader.** Open with a general overview of the pattern ("in most cases...") or look back at something the reader needs: the aim, the prediction, earlier findings, or the method. Method detail that the Methods section left out may appear here beside the result it explains.
2. **Show and compare.** Point to the figure or table, then report key results in a logical order, with comparisons to predictions, models, or earlier work. Add a brief direct explanation where the data support one.
3. **Acknowledge problems.** State anomalies, uncertainty, and incomplete or unexpected results with their size and scope, a likely reason, and a way forward. See [limitations and future work](../limitations-future-work/SKILL.md).
4. **Note implications.** Late in the section, one restrained sentence on what the results suggest signals the move to the Discussion.

## Procedure

- Order results so the ones that underpin others come first, not simply the most exciting first.
- Give an overview of the pattern before individual values, and repeat it when you move to a new set of results.
- Describe a few results in detail: those that are key, typical, or especially interesting. Describing everything equally makes everything look equally important. A result called "typical" must actually be typical.
- Mark an important or unexpected result with a new paragraph and an early signal such as "However" or "Although". See [signalling](../signalling-transitions/SKILL.md).
- Invite the reader to look at a figure or table at the right time. If the display can be read more than one way, state your reading first, then point to it. See [figure and table commentary](../figure-table-commentary/SKILL.md).
- Pair numbers with a frame the reader can use: a comparator, a prediction, a threshold. See [quantity](../quantity-comparisons/SKILL.md) and [frequency](../frequency-language/SKILL.md).
- Separate explanation (why it happened), evaluation (what the numbers mean), and implication (what it suggests). Keep broad explanations for the Discussion.
- Choose the tense of an interpretation deliberately: past simple ties it to this study; present simple presents it as established. See [tense](../tense-selection/SKILL.md).
- Calibrate causal and tentative wording with [causality](../causal-language/SKILL.md) and [claim strength](../claim-strength/SKILL.md). More wording in the [phrase bank](references/results-language.md).

## Guardrails

Do not create numbers, significance, or figure references. Do not drop a contrary result. Do not use selective emphasis or "typical" to hide a pattern. If the data are not shown, say so and be cautious about relying on them.

## Check

Does each claim trace to supplied data? Do the text and the display agree? Are key and contrary results visible, with uncertainty and exclusions reported? Is each sentence clearly an observation, an evaluation, or an interpretation? Does the section answer the aims stated in the Introduction?

**Illustrative example:** "In most cases, calibration reduced error. Median error fell from 12% to 7% at two of three sites (Fig. 2), in line with the manufacturer's specification. However, the third site showed no change, possibly because of its higher humidity."
