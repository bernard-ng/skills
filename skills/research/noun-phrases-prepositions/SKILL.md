# Noun phrases and prepositions

Compact noun strings are tempting and English tolerates them, but readers can parse them more than one way. Prepositions carry real meaning, and a title or caption has little context to rescue them.

## Procedure

1. In a noun string, the last noun is the head, and the earlier nouns modify it. Paraphrase the relation of every pair of nouns: made of, used for, caused by, located in, produced by.
2. Break up strings of three or more nouns, or any string with more than one possible reading. Use a preposition, a verb, or a relative clause ("training for repair technicians" instead of a long compound).
3. Use hyphens to show a modifier unit ("low-cost sensors", "long-term stability") and avoid ambiguous strings such as "heat treatment temperature effects".
4. Check each preposition for its sense:
   - **with** can mean using or having.
   - **for** and **of** after "evidence": evidence for X supports X; evidence of X is an observable sign of X.
   - **in** versus **from**: "code phase measures from receivers" (originating there) differs from "in receivers" (made inside them).
   - **of** can mark the agent or the object ("analysis of students" could be done by or on students).
   - **by**, **on**, **to**, and **across** each change the relation.
5. Prefer clearer structures to preposition-heavy ones, and ask a field reader to check titles.
6. Check that singular and plural modifiers read naturally ("sensor array", "samples preparation" is wrong).

## Guardrails

Do not remove a preposition or reorder nouns without confirming the intended relation. Do not expand a technical term of art that readers expect unchanged.

## Check

Can each noun-noun and preposition link be paraphrased in one way only?

**Illustrative example:** "Sensor drift measurement protocol evaluation" is unclear. "Evaluation of a protocol for measuring sensor drift" states the relations.
