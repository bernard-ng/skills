# Figure and table commentary

**Inputs:** the display, source data, labels, units, statistical definitions, and the comparison the reader should notice.

## Procedure

1. Identify the one or two findings each display supports, including contrary or null results that matter.
2. Decide the order of explanation and callout:
   - If the display has one clear reading, point to it, then comment.
   - If it can be read in more than one way, state your reading first, then point to the display.
3. Write the finding in prose with a callout. Do not leave the reader to infer it. Use the [Results](../write-results/SKILL.md), [quantity](../quantity-comparisons/SKILL.md), and [evaluative language](../evaluative-language/SKILL.md) guidance. Bare numbers invite the reader to form a different opinion.
4. Choose callout verbs that fit the display: a table lists or summarises, a graph plots or shows, a diagram illustrates, a figure compares. "As seen from Fig. 2" means deduced from the figure; "as seen in Fig. 2" means it appears there.
5. In captions, define groups, conditions, units, error bars, abbreviations, and statistical marks needed to read the display alone.
6. Describe only selected results in detail: important, typical, or especially interesting ones.
7. Cross-check every number, direction, label, denominator, and panel reference against the display. Flag mismatches.
8. Treat "data not shown" cautiously: say why and do not build a key claim on it.

## Check

Text and display must agree, the comparison that matters must be clear, and the uncertainty must be visible. Do not invent values, significance, or labels.

**Illustrative pattern:** "Median error fell from 12% to 7% after calibration (Fig. 2), with no change at site C."
