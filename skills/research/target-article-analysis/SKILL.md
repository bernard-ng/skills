# Target article analysis

Conventions differ by field and journal: section names, whether the Introduction previews results, passive or active voice, abstract format, how detailed Methods are. Find them from evidence rather than assuming.

## Choose target articles

Ask for, or help select, three or four recent research articles that:

- Come from the target journal or very similar ones, on a topic and study type close to the user's.
- Are original research with clear Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion or Conclusion sections (not textbook chapters or broad reviews). Headings vary (Procedure, Materials and Methods, Experimental, Analysis); use the journal's names.
- Are fairly short and, if possible, written by fluent English writers.

Also obtain the journal's author guide: section names and order, voice rules, word and figure limits, abstract format, units, and reference style.

## Procedure

1. **Skim each article** before reading closely: title, authors, date, abstract, first paragraph, first sentence of each paragraph, figure and table titles, last paragraph.
2. **Label each sentence's job, not its content.** Write "the writer states a gap", not "the writer says the polymer is weak". Avoid content words so the label transfers to other papers. Two tests help: imagine the sentence deleted and ask what the reader loses; and read the clues, such as the tense, connectors, and key verbs.
3. **Merge labels into four or five components** per section. Note the usual order, which components are optional, and which can repeat.
4. **Compare across articles.** Tabulate what is common and what varies: Does the Introduction end with the aim, method, or finding? Is Methods passive? Are limitations in Methods or Results? How long is the Abstract and what does it contain?
5. **Extract language by function**: verbs for reporting prior work, gap signals, ways of pointing to figures, justification phrases, strength words, applications. Record grammar patterns too (tense, voice, preposition). Write original sentences using these patterns; do not copy text.
6. **Record journal decisions**: voice, headings, tense, abbreviations, reporting of statistics, citation placement.
7. **Apply** the model to the user's draft: compare move maps and note missing or misplaced moves.

## Output

A compact convention sheet: section order and headings, the move model per section with optional components, tense and voice norms, language bank by function, and a list of unknowns for the user to resolve.

## Guardrails

Treat sample conventions as evidence, not rules; the author guide wins. If no samples are available, say which defaults you assumed and ask for samples. Do not invent articles or cite ones you have not seen. Do not reproduce passages from sample articles.

## Check

Is the model built from several articles, not one? Is every move labelled by function? Are differences between journals recorded rather than blended?
