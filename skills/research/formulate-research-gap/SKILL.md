# Formulate a research gap

**Inputs:** the study aim, the supporting sources and what each tested, and the intended contribution.

## Forms a gap can take

- A problem or limitation in earlier work (accuracy, cost, assumptions, scope).
- Something not yet studied (a population, setting, timescale, mechanism, or condition).
- A conflict between studies.
- A prediction or hypothesis that the paper will test.

Professional Introductions state these as statements, not as questions.

## Procedure

1. Write one sentence on what is known, with citations.
2. State the precise boundary: population, setting, method, mechanism, timescale, outcome, or contradiction that remains. Distinguish an evidence gap from a practical problem or a prediction.
3. Signal it early in the sentence with a contrast word ("However", "Although", "Despite"), so the reader recognises its function straight away.
4. Choose bounded wording. "Remains uncertain in outdoor deployments" is safer than "no study has examined".
5. Soften criticism of earlier work by describing limits of the method or scope, not faults of the authors. See [vocabulary](../academic-vocabulary/SKILL.md).
6. Connect the gap to a question the present design can answer, then state the aim with the [Introduction](../write-introduction/SKILL.md) model. Some background about materials or devices may be needed between the gap and the aim.
7. Remember that the Discussion will say how far the study answered this gap, so write the gap in terms that can be answered.

## Check

The gap must follow from evidence, matter to the reader, and match the study's actual scope. Do the aim, method, and conclusions answer it? If coverage is incomplete, mark the gap as tentative and name the literature check needed. Do not claim "first", "never", or "no studies" without a suitable search.

**Illustrative pattern:** "Existing calibration studies used controlled environments; performance during season-long outdoor deployment remains uncertain. We therefore tested..."
