# Literature review positioning

**Inputs:** the paper's research question, verified sources with their design and finding, and the journal's citation style. If sources are not supplied, ask for them or mark the passage provisional.

The literature part of an Introduction is a research map: it shows readers who the key contributors are, what has been achieved, and where this study sits. It is short, selective, and aimed at the question.

## Procedure

1. Choose the question the map must lead to, then select the sources that bear on it.
2. Group studies by one principle, and say which:
   - **Chronological**, when the field's development matters.
   - **By approach, theory, or method**, which avoids a back-and-forth "however" pattern.
   - **General to specific**, narrowing toward the closest work.
3. For each group, state what is established, what is disputed, and what the evidence does not cover. Attribute each claim to the source that supports it.
4. Pick reporting verbs that match what each study did and how strongly it concluded. See the [verb bank](../write-introduction/references/introduction-language.md) and [vocabulary](../academic-vocabulary/SKILL.md).
5. Use tense deliberately: past simple for one completed study, present perfect for accumulated or still-relevant work, present simple for accepted facts. See [tense](../tense-selection/SKILL.md).
6. Place each citation after the exact claim it supports, mid-sentence if the sentence mixes sources. See [citation attribution](../citation-attribution/SKILL.md).
7. Finish at the gap. Carry the same groups forward into the Discussion so the comparison there uses studies the reader already knows. See [contribution mapping](../contribution-mapping/SKILL.md).

## Check

Can a reader see why each cited study matters to this question? Do quantifiers such as "most studies" match the actual source set? Is it clear which statements are cited work and which are the present study? Flag missing coverage, incompatible measures, and uncited claims. Never invent a citation or treat a selection as an exhaustive review.

**Illustrative pattern:** "Controlled studies report improved calibration accuracy, whereas the few field studies leave long-term stability uncertain." Use only after verifying both groups of evidence.
