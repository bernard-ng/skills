# Abstract

**Inputs:** the finished paper, journal format and word limit, and the one or two results that justify the paper.

An abstract is read far more often than the paper and often alone, in databases. It must stand by itself, persuade the right readers to get the paper, and contain nothing the paper does not. Write it after the paper is stable, assembling and shortening material from the body rather than inventing text.

## Components

1. Background, aim, problem, or what the paper does.
2. Method or materials.
3. Results, achievement or contribution, and implications.
4. Applications.
5. Limitations or future work (rare, brief, and only if important).

## Two shapes

- **Structured or expanded:** most components in order, often under headings such as Background, Methods, Results, Conclusions. Words are spread fairly evenly. Background is one or two sentences at most.
- **Compact:** two or three components, usually method plus results, with the other components merged into the same sentences. Method generally comes before the result it explains. This is the more common form in narrow fields.

The journal usually decides. Where the choice is free, narrow specialist fields lean compact and broader fields lean structured. See [patterns and tense](references/abstract-patterns.md).

## Procedure

1. Pick the components needed to make the paper findable and credible. Write them in the paper's own terms.
2. Merge components to save words: aim plus method, achievement plus method, problem plus method.
3. Give specific results. Avoid "better", "small", or "various methods". Add the benchmark that makes a number interpretable (a comparator, "only 38%", "up to 15%").
4. Match tense to the job of each sentence. Gap and what the paper does are present simple; method is usually past; results are past or present; achievements are present perfect or present; applications use present simple or modals. See [tense](../tense-selection/SKILL.md).
5. State results and implications directly, but keep them consistent with the paper. Use "may" or "could" when the finding is early-stage or the implication is not firm.
6. Include the exact terms readers would search for, once each, in natural phrasing.
7. Define each abbreviation at first use, and avoid citations unless the paper directly extends or contradicts a specific work. See [conventions](../scientific-conventions/SKILL.md).
8. Cut to the limit, usually a single paragraph of about 80 to 250 words. Draft long, then remove inessential words and sentences. Do not go over the limit.

## Guardrails

Do not add a result, number, or conclusion absent from the paper. Do not compress away a qualification that changes the claim. Do not write "is discussed" or "will be described" in place of the content. Mention a problem only if it materially affects use of the results.

## Check

Does it contain the actual aim, method, main evidence, and bounded conclusion? Is every statement traceable to the paper? Does it satisfy format and word limit? Would an indexing reader know what was studied, how, and what was found?

**Illustrative example:** "We evaluated drift in six low-cost air monitors over a 12-week outdoor deployment. Median absolute error increased from 6 to 11 units, mostly after week 8. These findings suggest that calibration intervals may need to be shorter for extended deployments." Replace every fact with supplied evidence.
