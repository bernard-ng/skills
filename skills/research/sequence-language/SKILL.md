# Sequence

Readers need order and also timing, so they can repeat a method or compare results. "Then" gives order only. It does not say how long each step took or how soon the next followed.

## Procedure

1. Sketch a timeline of the verified chronology and note any simultaneous steps.
2. Place each event in one of these groups, and use wording that matches:
   - **Before the study began** (previously, beforehand, prior to).
   - **Start** (at first, initially, to begin with).
   - **Order only** (then, next, afterwards, once). Add durations if the interval matters.
   - **Short interval** (soon, shortly after, immediately).
   - **Long interval or late** (later, eventually, towards the end).
   - **Same time** (when, as, while, meanwhile, simultaneously, upon + -ing).
   - **End** (finally, at the end, lastly).
   - **After the study** (afterwards, post-analysis, in a follow-up).
3. Give explicit time values where the interval matters ("after 24 h at room temperature").
4. Choose the signal that matches the facts: "after" for order, "during" for overlap, "when" for near simultaneity.

## Guardrails

Do not turn a list of steps into a causal chain: simultaneous or immediately following events may be read as cause and effect ("The temperature dropped sharply when pressure was reduced"). "While" may suggest contrast. Avoid "subsequently" when order is unknown. If two actions are simultaneous, do not write one as following the other.

## Check

Could a reader reproduce the sequence, or align results with the correct phase?

**Illustrative example:** "Before deployment, each device was calibrated. During week 1, readings were logged hourly. After retrieval, missing intervals were flagged."
