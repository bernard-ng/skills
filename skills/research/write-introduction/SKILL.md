# Introduction

**Inputs:** topic and significance, essential definitions, relevant verified citations, the gap or problem, the aim, and the approach and main finding if the journal previews them. Draft it after Methods and Results exist, so it describes the study that was actually done.

## Four-move model

The Introduction narrows from the field to this paper. Most introductions begin with move 1 and end with move 4. Moves 2 and 3 can repeat.

1. **Establish importance and background.** Open with something every reader will accept: why the field matters, how much work it attracts, or a widely accepted fact. If stuck, define a key word from the title. Add the facts and definitions needed to understand the problem area, then state the current research focus briefly.
2. **Situate prior and current research.** Give a short, organised account of who did what, as the research map the reader needs. See [literature positioning](../literature-review-positioning/SKILL.md).
3. **Locate the gap.** Show a problem, a limitation, an unanswered question, or a prediction to be tested, and why it follows from the cited work. Signal it early in the sentence ("However", "Although"). State it as a statement or hypothesis, not as a question. See [research gap](../formulate-research-gap/SKILL.md).
4. **Describe the present paper.** Give its purpose, approach, and, if conventional, structure and headline finding. Make "this paper" or "we" the visible actor.

## Procedure

- Write a one-line move map per paragraph; put information in the order readers need it, general first (overview before details).
- Do not open with the problem or the aim. Readers need background before they can judge a problem. A short problem statement tends to grow into detail the reader is not ready for.
- Match background depth to the readership. A broad journal needs more definitions and basics than a narrow one. When unsure, include slightly more background rather than less, but keep it proportional to the question.
- Cite background facts that come from research. A very well-known fact needs no citation; a recent problem does.
- Order prior work by one clear principle: chronology, approach or model, or general-to-specific. Avoid a back-and-forth run of alternating "however" sentences.
- Present simple suits accepted background. Past simple or present perfect suits named studies. Present perfect also marks research that still bears on now and a gap that still exists. Past simple would say the gap existed then. See [tense](../tense-selection/SKILL.md).
- State the aim in past simple, or present simple if the paper reports only part of it. Describe the paper itself in present simple ("This paper tests...").
- Preview the method or finding only enough to guide the reader; do not exhaust Methods or Results.
- Soften criticism of prior work with impersonal, calibrated wording. See [vocabulary](../academic-vocabulary/SKILL.md) and the [phrase bank](references/introduction-language.md).
- The last sentences connect to Methods. The Discussion later reverses this sequence.

## Guardrails

Do not manufacture a gap, consensus, citation, or novelty claim. Do not claim that nothing is known without a stated search. Keep journal-specific structure when it differs from this model.

## Check

Can a reader say why the topic matters, what prior work established, what remains unresolved, and what this paper does? Is every gap and novelty claim supported? Does each citation sit next to the claim it supports? Do the aims here match the Methods, Results, and Conclusion?

**Illustrative move sequence:** "Compact air monitors are increasingly used for neighborhood exposure studies." (importance) → "Recent calibration studies improved their accuracy in controlled conditions." (research) → "Their stability during prolonged outdoor use remains uncertain." (gap) → "We therefore assessed six monitors over one season." (this paper).
