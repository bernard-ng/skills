# Frequency

Without a frequency, readers cannot repeat a method or judge a result. How often an effect occurred also tells readers how reliable it is.

## Procedure

1. Prefer an exact count or rate when you have one: "in 7 of 12 trials", "on three days", "per cycle". Name the denominator and the observation window.
2. If a word is needed, choose the level deliberately.

| Level | Wording |
| --- | --- |
| Every time | always, without exception, on every occasion |
| Almost always | generally, normally, usually, as a rule |
| Recurring | regularly, repeatedly |
| Common | frequently, often |
| Slightly more than half | more often than not |
| Neutral | as often as not |
| Occasional | sometimes, at times, on some occasions |
| Infrequent | occasionally, now and then, from time to time |
| Rare | rarely, seldom, infrequently |
| Almost never | hardly ever, almost never |
| Never | never, on no occasion, not once |

3. Remember that frequency words are partly subjective. "Often" can reflect how often the event was expected, so an 18% rate of a rare event may be called frequent. If that is the author's interpretation, say what it is measured against. See [evaluative language](../evaluative-language/SKILL.md).
4. In Methods, give frequencies for repeated steps ("washed three times") so the procedure can be repeated.
5. Match the tense to the observation period.

## Guardrails

"Regularly" suggests a pattern of intervals as well as recurrence. "Frequently" and "often" have no fixed threshold. Never substitute a stronger frequency term to make a result more persuasive. "Never" requires complete observation.

## Check

Does the wording match the numerator, denominator, and window? Is "never" warranted?

**Illustrative example:** "The alarm activated in 7 of 12 trials" is clearer than "The alarm often activated."
